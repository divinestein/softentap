What Size Water Softener Do You Need? The Grain Math, Worked Out

Size a water softener from your hardness, household water use and iron: the grains-per-day formula, two worked examples for a family of four, and why oversizing wastes water.

Short answer: multiply people × gallons per person per day × hardness in gpg to get the grains your softener must remove each day, then multiply by the number of days you want between regenerations. Aim for about three days, and pick the smallest unit whose capacity at an efficient salt setting covers that number. Bigger is not better: the EPA warns that oversizing wastes water.

The worked examples below use a family of four. One uses WaterOne’s published average of 7.9 gpg from our hardness report guide; the other is a clearly labeled hypothetical well at 15 gpg with iron.

Key takeaways

  • Daily grains = people × gallons per person × gpg. The WQA suggests 70 gallons per person as a starting point; your water bill is better.
  • Add dissolved iron as “compensated hardness” (WQA example factor: 3 gpg per ppm).
  • Size for about three days between regenerations (WQA), and do not oversize (EPA).
  • A “32,000-grain” label is often measured at a heavy salt dose. Compare capacity at the salt setting you will actually use, and look for 3,350+ grains per pound of salt.

How we sourced this

We have not tested any softener. The method and rules of thumb come from the Water Quality Association’s sizing article, EPA WaterSense, and NDSU Extension. The worked examples are our own arithmetic on those published figures. Your dealer or manufacturer capacity table should have the final word.

The sizing formula

Grains per day = people × gallons per person per day × hardness (gpg)

Capacity needed = grains per day × days between regenerations

A softener’s grain rating is how much hardness it can remove before it has to regenerate. EPA WaterSense gives the example that a 32,000-grain softener can remove 32,000 grains of hardness in a cycle, and says both your hardness and your household water use determine the size you need.

If your report gives hardness in ppm or mg/L, divide by 17.1 to get gpg. Our hardness converter does it for you.

Step 1: estimate your water use

The Water Quality Association’s sizing guide suggests 70 gallons per person per day as a starting point. It derives that from a 1999 EPA estimate of 400 gallons per day for a family of four, 70% of it indoors. EPA WaterSense’s current figure is that the average American family uses more than 300 gallons a day at home, with roughly 70% indoors. That works out to a little over 200 gallons of indoor use for a family, so 70 gallons per person is a generous estimate rather than a tight one.

Both the EPA and the WQA point to the same better input: your own water bill. Many utilities bill in cubic feet or hundreds of cubic feet; one cubic foot is about 7.48 gallons. Leave out outdoor use. The EPA recommends plumbing the softener after the line to hose bibs and irrigation where possible, and the WQA notes softened water should not be used for irrigation.

Step 2: add iron if you have it

Dissolved (ferrous) iron takes up exchange sites on the resin just like calcium and magnesium. Sizing guides handle it with compensated hardness: multiply the iron in ppm by a factor and add it to the hardness. The WQA says the factor is typically 3 but may be 2, 4 or 5, and uses 3 in its example (10 gpg + 3 ppm iron × 3 = 19 gpg). Use whatever factor your softener’s manual specifies.

Caution

Iron is where softener sizing goes wrong. The WQA says that in water above pH 6.5, iron oxidizes more easily and fouls resin, and resin companies recommend keeping iron treated by a softener under 3 ppm. Above that, it points to separate oxidation and filtration. NDSU Extension says supplies above 10 ppm iron and manganese may need a dedicated iron removal system. If you are on a well, test for iron, manganese and pH before you size anything.

Step 3: pick days between regenerations

The WQA recommends choosing a resin amount that gives about three days of capacity between regenerations to help reduce water use. It also says capacity has to be balanced against tank size so the tank is not oversized for the home’s flow rate, and that a smaller dual-tank unit is sometimes a better fit than one large tank.

EPA WaterSense is blunt about the other direction: “It is important not to oversize your system, as this will lead to inefficient use of water.” It also recommends demand-initiated regeneration (a meter or hardness sensor) over a fixed timer, because a timer regenerates whether or not the resin is spent.

Read the grain rating carefully

Softeners are sold by a single grain number, but capacity depends on how much salt each regeneration uses. A WaterWorld trade article explains that a common 1 cubic foot “32,000-grain” rating is based on roughly 15 lb of salt, which works out to only about 2,133 grains removed per pound. At a lower salt setting, the same unit removes about 24,000 grains with nearly twice the salt efficiency.

So when you compare units, ask for the capacity at the salt setting you plan to use, not just the headline number. The EPA suggests checking two efficiency figures, both verifiable through the voluntary efficiency rating under NSF/ANSI 44:

  • 5 gallons of water or less per 1,000 grains of hardness removed.
  • Salt efficiency greater than 3,350 grains of hardness per pound of salt.

For scale, the EPA says a typical softener uses 20 to 70 gallons of water per regeneration cycle, and some larger systems up to 200.

A short checklist before you buy

  1. Get a hardness number from your utility’s water quality report, or a lab test if you are on a well (include iron, manganese and pH).
  2. Get daily indoor use from your water bill, or start from 70 gallons per person.
  3. Compute compensated hardness, grains per day, and capacity for about three days.
  4. Pick the smallest unit that covers it at an efficient salt setting. Prefer demand-initiated regeneration and NSF/ANSI 44 certification.
  5. Check local rules and septic compatibility. The EPA notes some jurisdictions restrict softeners.

If the math says you need very little capacity, compare the other options in Softener, conditioner, filter or RO? before you buy.
