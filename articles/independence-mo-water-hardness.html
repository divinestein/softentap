Independence, MO Water Hardness: What the Courtney Bend Numbers Mean for a Softener

Independence’s 2025 water report lists total hardness of 123 mg/L, about 7.2 grains per gallon, after softening at the Courtney Bend plant. How to read it, the sodium and pH lines, and when a home softener makes sense.

The City of Independence Water Department’s 2025 Water Quality Report lists total hardness of 123 mg/L as calcium carbonate. That is about 7.2 grains per gallon, just over the line from “moderately hard” into “hard” on the USGS scale. The report also says the well water is softened at the Courtney Bend treatment plant before it reaches homes, so what comes out of an Independence tap has already had part of its hardness removed. For many homes, a whole-house softener here is a comfort choice, not a necessity.

How we sourced this

We did not test Independence tap water. Every figure here comes from the City of Independence 2025 Water Quality Report (Consumer Confidence Report, or CCR) for system MO1010399, the state-formatted version of that report on the Missouri Department of Natural Resources CCR site, and the USGS, EPA and university-extension sources listed at the end. Where a number is our arithmetic or estimate, we say so.

Key takeaways

  • Independence reports total hardness of 123 mg/L. Divided by 17.1, that is about 7.2 grains per gallon, at the low end of “hard.”
  • The water comes from Missouri River alluvial wells and is softened and disinfected at the Courtney Bend plant.
  • The hardness, sodium and pH figures come from a single sample dated May 6, 2024. Treat them as a snapshot, and test your own tap before programming a softener.
  • The water already carries 50 mg/L of sodium. A home softener would add more, so leave a drinking tap unsoftened if sodium matters to you.
  • A softener does nothing for lead. Lead results were well under the action level, but a certified filter is the tool if you are concerned.

The published hardness number

The “Additional Parameters” table in the city’s 2025 report lists Hardness, Total (mg/L as Calcium Carbonate): 123. The Missouri DNR version of the same report shows the same 123 mg/L, labeled “Hardness, carbonate,” with a collection date of May 6, 2024. It is one sample, not a yearly average or range.

Converted with the usual factor (WaterOne, the Johnson County utility, tells customers to divide ppm by 17.1):

123 mg/L ÷ 17.1 = 7.2 grains per gallon (our arithmetic)

The same table breaks the hardness into its two minerals: calcium 16.4 mg/L and magnesium 19.9 mg/L. If you run a home test kit that reports calcium hardness only, expect it to read well below 123 mg/L, because much of Independence’s remaining hardness is magnesium. Use a total-hardness test.

Why it is already partly softened

According to the report, Independence draws its water from wells at the Courtney Bend Water Treatment Plant that tap the Missouri River alluvial aquifer, which the state classifies as groundwater. (The report’s opening letter says 44 wells; the system description says 42.) “The well water is softened and disinfected at the treatment plant,” the report says. The plant can supply up to 48 million gallons a day and serves about 250,000 people, including Independence residents and 12 wholesale customers.

That last point matters if you do not live in Independence. Other systems that buy Independence water print its results in their own reports. Kansas City’s 2025 report, for example, carries the same 123 mg/L line in its “Reseller Secondary Contaminants” table under the name Independence PWS. Our Kansas City, MO hardness guide explains why that line should not be read as KC Water’s own number. If your report shows an Independence line, ask your utility which supply actually reaches your address.

Do you need a softener on Independence water?

At about 7.2 gpg, Independence sits right at the thresholds extension services use:

  • Virginia Cooperative Extension says levels under 7.0 gpg (120 mg/L) generally do not require treatment, and that treatment makes sense when hardness is actually causing a problem.
  • NDSU Extension, citing NSF International, says consumers tend to see a problem once hardness passes about 6 to 7 grains per gallon.

So the honest answer is: not required, and borderline as a comfort purchase. If spotting on glassware and fixtures, scale in the kettle, or soap use bothers you, a softener will reduce it. If none of that bothers you, the plant has already done much of the work. If you decide to install one:

  • Size from the real number. Program the softener from your tested hardness, not a rounded-up guess. Our softener sizing guide walks through the grain math.
  • Read the near-8 gpg case. The trade-offs in Do you need a softener at about 8 gpg? apply almost unchanged at 7.2 gpg.
  • Use the right tool. If your main complaint is taste, odor or what is in your drinking water, a softener is the wrong device. See softener vs conditioner vs filter vs RO.

Sodium and pH: two lines to read before softening

Two other figures from the same May 2024 sample are worth knowing before you add a home softener:

  • Sodium: 50 mg/L. The water already contains sodium before any home treatment. NDSU Extension puts the sodium a salt-based softener adds at about 8 mg/L per grain of hardness removed. Removing 7.2 gpg would add roughly 58 mg/L, for a total near 108 mg/L in fully softened water (our estimate from NDSU’s figure and the report’s 50 mg/L). If anyone in the home watches sodium, the common answer is to leave the kitchen cold tap unsoftened.
  • pH: 9.65. The DNR version of the report lists this against a secondary guideline of 8.5. EPA’s secondary standards (a range of 6.5–8.5 for pH) cover taste, odor and appearance; they are non-enforceable, and utilities are not required to meet them.

The same table lists alkalinity of 60.2 mg/L, chloride 30 mg/L, sulfate 144 mg/L (guideline 250) and total dissolved solids of 322 mg/L (guideline 500). The water is disinfected with chloramine; the report lists a range of 1.5–2.64 ppm and an average of 1.89 ppm, against a maximum residual disinfectant level of 4 ppm.

Lead: the line a softener does not fix

For Lead and Copper Rule testing in 2023–2025, the report lists a 90th-percentile lead result of 1.1 ppb, with a range of 0–9.08 ppb, against an action level of 15 ppb, and no sites over the action level. The city reports no violations for 2025.

Lead in tap water usually comes from service lines and home plumbing, not the treatment plant, so results vary from house to house. The Missouri DNR version of the report advises anyone concerned to flush the tap before use and to consider a filter certified by an ANSI-accredited certifier to reduce lead. A water softener is not a lead filter. If you buy a filter for lead, confirm that the specific model is certified for lead reduction.

When to test your own tap

Because the hardness figure is a single sample from May 2024, a quick check at your own kitchen tap is worth doing before buying equipment. Test unsoftened, cold water, and use a kit that reads total hardness rather than calcium hardness alone. A lab test is the firmer basis if you plan to program a softener precisely.

  • Not every Independence-area address is on city water. Neighboring districts publish their own CCRs. Find yours through EPA’s Consumer Confidence Report page and read it the way our hardness-report guide explains.
  • Private wells have no CCR at all. A utility number says nothing about a well.
