Kansas City, MO Water Hardness: What KC Water’s Numbers Mean for a Softener

KC Water lists average hardness of 100 mg/L, about 5.8 grains per gallon, after lime softening at its plant. How to read that number, the CCR line to avoid, and when a softener makes sense.

KC Water, the city utility for Kansas City, Missouri, publishes an average hardness of 100 mg/L, which it describes as 5–6 grains per gallon. That is moderately hard water, and it is lower than many people expect for a Missouri River city because KC Water already softens the water with lime at its treatment plant. For most homes on KC Water, a whole-house softener is a comfort choice, not a necessity.

How we sourced this

We did not test Kansas City tap water. Every figure here comes from KC Water’s own website and treatment documents, the 2025 Annual Water Quality Report (Consumer Confidence Report, or CCR) for Kansas City PWS (system MO1010415), and the USGS and university-extension sources listed at the end. Where a number is our arithmetic, we say so.

Key takeaways

  • KC Water states an average hardness of 100 mg/L. Divided by 17.1, that is about 5.8 grains per gallon, “moderately hard” on the USGS scale.
  • KC Water adds lime at its plant for softening, so the water reaching homes is already partly softened.
  • The only hardness line in the 2025 CCR (123 mg/L) belongs to Independence PWS, an upstream seller, not to KC Water. Do not set a softener from it.
  • Extension guidance generally treats hardness under about 7 gpg as not needing treatment unless it is causing a problem you care about.

KC Water’s published hardness number

On its Water Quality Topics page, KC Water says: “The average hardness of KC Water is 100 mg/L (5-6 grains/gallon).” The same page explains that hardness comes from calcium and magnesium, that it does not cause health problems, and that it can leave water spots on dishes, stainless steel sinks and faucets.

Converted with the usual factor (WaterOne, the Johnson County utility next door, tells customers to divide ppm by 17.1):

100 mg/L ÷ 17.1 = 5.8 grains per gallon (our arithmetic; it falls inside KC Water’s own 5–6 gpg band)

Two limits on that number: the page gives a single average, with no range and no year. Hardness from a river supply moves with the seasons, so your tap may read somewhat above or below 100 mg/L on a given day.

Why Kansas City water is already partly softened

KC Water draws its water from the Missouri River. Its treatment-process sheet describes a softening and disinfection step in which “lime is added for softening and for improving taste and odor,” and its tap-water FAQ says the utility “disinfects, softens, and stabilizes the water” before filtering and pumping it citywide. In other words, part of the hardness is removed before the water reaches your meter.

For comparison, WaterOne’s 2026 report lists an average total hardness of 135 ppm (about 7.9 gpg) for its Johnson County, Kansas, service area. If you have moved from Overland Park or Leawood to Kansas City, Missouri, expect somewhat softer water. The WaterOne report walkthrough shows those numbers in detail.

The CCR trap: the 123 mg/L line is not KC Water

If you open the 2025 Annual Water Quality Report for Kansas City PWS and search for “hardness,” you will find one line: Hardness, carbonate — 123 mg/L. It is easy to take that as Kansas City’s number. It is not.

  • That line sits in a table titled Reseller Secondary Contaminants, and its Water System Name column says Independence PWS, with a single sample dated May 6, 2024. Our Independence, MO hardness guide covers that supply in detail.
  • The report’s buyer and seller table shows Kansas City PWS buying water from Jackson County PWSD 2, which in turn buys from Independence PWS. That is why the other system’s results appear in Kansas City’s report.
  • Kansas City’s own Secondary Contaminants table lists alkalinity, chloride, pH, sulfate, total dissolved solids and others, but no hardness row.

The CCR does not say which neighborhoods, if any, receive that purchased water. If you want to be sure which supply serves your address, KC Water is the one to ask. For setting a softener, the utility’s published 100 mg/L average, or your own test, is the better starting point than a single reseller sample.

Do you need a softener on KC Water?

At about 5.8 gpg, KC Water is below the common thresholds used by extension services:

  • Virginia Cooperative Extension says levels under 7.0 gpg (120 mg/L) generally do not require treatment, and that treatment makes sense when hardness is actually causing a problem.
  • NDSU Extension, citing NSF International, says consumers tend to see a problem once hardness passes about 6 to 7 grains per gallon.

So the plain answer for most KC Water homes is: you do not need one. You might still want one if spotting on glass and fixtures, or soap use, bothers you enough to justify the cost and maintenance. Before buying, weigh the following:

  • Sodium. KC Water itself notes that many softeners swap calcium and magnesium for sodium, and that drinking softened water can raise sodium intake. NDSU puts the added sodium at about 8 mg/L per grain removed. At 5.8 gpg, that works out to roughly 46 mg/L of added sodium (our estimate from NDSU’s figure). A common answer is to leave the kitchen cold tap unsoftened.
  • Sizing. If you do install one, size and program it from your real hardness and water use, not a rounded-up guess. Our softener sizing guide walks through the grain math.
  • Alternatives. If your main complaint is taste or what is in your drinking water, a softener is the wrong tool. See softener vs conditioner vs filter vs RO.

If a home test reads closer to 8 gpg, the reasoning in Do you need a softener at about 8 gpg? applies.

When to test your own tap

Because KC Water publishes one average without a range or date, a quick check at your own kitchen tap is worth doing before spending money on equipment. Test unsoftened, cold water. Strip tests are fine for a rough check; a lab test is the firmer basis if you plan to program a softener precisely.

  • Suburbs are often not on KC Water. Many Kansas City–area cities have their own utility and their own CCR. Find yours through EPA’s Consumer Confidence Report page and read it the way our hardness-report guide explains.
  • Private wells have no CCR at all. A utility average says nothing about a well.

Other lines in the report worth reading

Hardness is a nuisance measure. A few other lines in the same 2025 report are more useful for deciding whether you need a filter instead of, or as well as, a softener:

  • Lead. The 90th-percentile lead result for 2023–2025 was 2.08 ppb, with a range of 0–5.4 ppb, against an action level of 15 ppb. The report also lists 2025 Lead and Copper Rule monitoring and consumer-notice violations, and advises anyone concerned to use a filter certified by an ANSI-accredited certifier to reduce lead. A softener is not a lead filter.
  • Dissolved minerals. Kansas City’s own secondary results list total dissolved solids of 340 mg/L (guideline 500), sulfate 178 mg/L (guideline 250), and total alkalinity of 158–216 mg/L. These are aesthetic measures, not health limits.
  • pH. The report lists a pH of 10.1 from a May 2022 sample, above EPA’s non-enforceable secondary guideline range of 6.5–8.5. Secondary standards cover taste, odor and appearance, and utilities are not required to meet them.
