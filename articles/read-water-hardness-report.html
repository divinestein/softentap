How to Read Your Water Utility’s Hardness Report

Find total hardness on a Consumer Confidence Report, convert ppm to grains per gallon, and follow a WaterOne example: 135 ppm is about 7.9 gpg.

If you are shopping for a softener or programming one you already own, the device will usually ask for hardness in grains per gallon (gpg). Your water utility’s annual report will usually list hardness in parts per million (ppm) or milligrams per liter (mg/L). Those two metric units are treated as the same for drinking water. Grains are not. This guide shows how to find the right line on the report and convert it, using WaterOne — the utility that serves Leawood and much of Johnson County, Kansas — as a fully worked example.

Key takeaways

  • Use the total hardness line reported “as CaCO3”, not the separate calcium or magnesium figures.
  • Divide ppm (or mg/L) by 17.1 to get grains per gallon, the unit softeners ask for.
  • WaterOne’s 2026 report (2025 data) lists an average of 135 ppm, about 7.9 gpg: hard on the USGS scale, not very hard.
  • Check the data year. A report is often titled one year ahead of the data it covers.

How we sourced this

We have not tested anyone’s tap water. The WaterOne figures below come from the utility’s published water quality reports. If you do not use WaterOne, use your own utility’s latest report and the same method.

What “hardness” means on a report

Hardness is mainly dissolved calcium and magnesium. Utilities report it as the equivalent amount of calcium carbonate (CaCO3). That “as CaCO3” note matters, because the same report may also list elemental calcium or magnesium in ppm, and those numbers are not the hardness figure you enter into a softener.

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency has not set a legal limit for hardness. The U.S. Geological Survey notes that calcium and magnesium are not treated as toxic contaminants in this context. Hardness is about scale, soap performance, and taste or feel — not a pass/fail safety score.

USGS classifies hardness, as calcium carbonate, as follows:

Some university and health-department tables insert a “slightly hard” band at the low end and still call 121–180 mg/L hard. The important part is the same: numbers around 8 gpg sit in the “hard” range, not “very hard.”

How to find your report

Community water systems in the United States must prepare an annual Consumer Confidence Report (CCR), sometimes titled a water quality report. Ways to get it:

  • Check the mailer, email, or bill insert from your utility, or the water-quality section of its website.
  • Use EPA’s Consumer Confidence Report page and its CCR search tool.
  • If you are on a private well, there is no CCR. You need a lab test; a utility average will not describe your well.

Read the cover or first pages carefully for two dates. The report year is often one year ahead of the data year. WaterOne’s 2026 Water Quality Report, for example, states that the data are from 2025, as required by drinking-water rules.

Which line to use

Look for total hardness, usually under unregulated or secondary parameters, and confirm it is reported as CaCO3. Use that average for a conversion unless the report clearly gives a better number for your pressure zone (most residential reports do not).

Do not use these in place of total hardness:

  • Calcium or magnesium listed as elements (for WaterOne in 2025 data, those averages were 29 ppm calcium and 12 ppm magnesium — not the hardness number).
  • Calcium hardness alone, if total hardness is also listed. WaterOne reported average calcium hardness of 79 ppm and average total hardness of 135 ppm in the same 2025 data set. A softener should be set from total hardness.
  • Alkalinity, total dissolved solids, or conductivity. Those are related mineral measures, not grains of hardness.

Convert ppm or mg/L to grains per gallon

WaterOne tells customers who are installing a softener to divide the most recent total hardness value in ppm by 17.1 to get grains per gallon. That is the conversion used by many state operator formula sheets, which list 1 grain per gallon as 17.1 mg/L. A more exact factor used in some engineering tables is 17.118 mg/L. A few extension publications round to 17. For setting a softener, 17.1 is the figure to use unless your utility publishes a different instruction.

grains per gallon = total hardness (ppm or mg/L as CaCO3) ÷ 17.1

Worked example: WaterOne, including Leawood

WaterOne is an independent public utility. Its published service area includes Leawood, Lenexa, Overland Park, Shawnee, Prairie Village, and a list of other Johnson County cities, plus some unincorporated areas. It is not part of Johnson County government, and it is not affiliated with this site.

In the 2026 Water Quality Report (data from calendar year 2025), WaterOne listed:

  • Total hardness, as CaCO3: average 135 ppm, range 110–157 ppm.
  • Calcium hardness, as CaCO3: average 79 ppm, range 64–120 ppm.

Using WaterOne’s own method:

  • 135 ÷ 17.1 = 7.9 grains per gallon (7.89, typically rounded to 7.9).
  • The year’s range, 110–157 ppm, is about 6.4 to 9.2 gpg.

On the USGS scale, 135 mg/L is hard water, just above the 121 mg/L lower bound for that class, and well below the “very hard” line at 180 mg/L.

Hardness is not a single frozen number. WaterOne’s previous reports show why you should pull the latest average rather than reuse a figure from memory:

If you are setting a softener today on WaterOne’s system, 7.9 gpg matches the latest published average. If you want a margin for the high end of the reported range, that is a separate choice; the utility’s written instruction is to use the most recent average divided by 17.1.

What this number does not tell you

A hardness conversion will not tell you whether you have lead, PFAS, chlorine taste, iron, or manganese problems. Those, if present, appear on other lines of the same report, or they require a targeted test — especially in homes with older plumbing, or on wells. A softener is not a substitute for a filter certified to reduce a specific health contaminant. For the difference between softeners, conditioners, filters, and reverse osmosis, see the comparison guide.

Strip-style home tests can be a useful check against a utility average, but they are less precise than a laboratory or utility method. If your house is on a well, or you have a reason to think your plumbing changes the water, a certified lab test is the firmer basis.

For whether about 8 gpg is enough reason to install a softener, see Do you need a softener at about 8 grains per gallon?
