Do You Need a Water Softener at About 8 Grains per Gallon?

Reviewed November 18, 2026. We have not tested products. Figures below come from cited public sources.

Short answer: not automatically. Water at about 8 grains per gallon (gpg) is hard on the usual U.S. Geological Survey scale, but it is not “very hard.” On many municipal supplies, including WaterOne in Johnson County, Kansas, the utility already treats the water, and a home softener is a comfort, soap, and scale-maintenance decision — not a requirement for safe drinking water.

This article uses ~8 gpg and WaterOne’s latest average of about 7.9 gpg as the example. If your number is much higher, or you are on a well with iron or manganese, the trade-offs change. Start by converting your own report before you treat this as your number.

We have not surveyed homes or tested softeners at 8 gpg. The decision framework below is built from USGS classifications, university extension guidance, NSF’s consumer threshold as reported by NDSU, and WaterOne’s own published advice.

Where 8 gpg sits on the scale

USGS calls water hard from 121 to 180 mg/L as calcium carbonate, and very hard above 180 mg/L. Using the standard 17.1 conversion, that hard band is about 7.1 to 10.5 gpg. Eight grains is inside it, closer to the moderate/hard boundary than to the very-hard line.

WaterOne’s 2026 Water Quality Report (2025 data) lists average total hardness of 135 ppm, which is 7.9 gpg, with a range of 110–157 ppm (about 6.4–9.2 gpg). WaterOne already softens at the plant with lime and soda ash, then filters. The utility tells customers that hardness is an aesthetic issue, that the tap is safe to drink without a home filter, and that a home softener is “a personal choice.”

Guidance that talks about a 7-grain neighborhood

Two independent sources put everyday “do I need treatment?” conversations around this same band — without turning it into a mandate:

  • Virginia Tech’s Household Water Quality Program has written that water hardness levels less than 7.0 gpg, or 120 mg/L, generally do not require treatment, and that treatment is necessary when hardness is causing a problem for the homeowner. At 7.9 gpg you are slightly above that “generally do not require” line, which is another way of saying the decision is about symptoms, not a rule.
  • North Dakota State University Extension, citing NSF International, reports that consumers believe they have a problem when hardness surpasses the 6 to 7 gpg level. NDSU also states that hardness removal is not a necessity to protect your health; softening is popular because people prefer the way softened water bathes, cleans, and washes. Consumption of hard water is not framed as a health problem there; the minerals can even contribute some dietary calcium and magnesium. USGS similarly notes that the World Health Organization has said drinking water may contribute calcium and magnesium, which can matter for people with low intake.

So 8 gpg is in the zone where people often start to notice soap and scale issues, and also in the zone where skipping a softener is still a reasonable choice if those issues are mild.

Signs a softener might be worth it at this level

Consider a softener more seriously if several of these are true in your house, not just because the report says “hard”:

  • Persistent white crust on faucets and shower heads, or scale you keep chipping off kettles and coffee makers.
  • Spots or film on glassware after the dishwasher, or laundry that looks dull even when you dose detergent correctly.
  • Skin or hair that feels coated or dry after showers, especially in winter (NDSU describes this as a common hard-water complaint).
  • A tankless or high-efficiency water heater, or a recirculating hot-water system. WaterOne notes that those devices can encourage mineral buildup, and that an overly hot tank setting is a common factor in calcium deposits.
  • You already buy a lot of extra soap, detergent, or bottled water specifically to work around the tap.

Reasons to wait, or to soften only part of the house

A whole-house softener is a purchase plus a long stream of salt, water for regeneration, and occasional service. At 8 gpg, those costs buy comfort and maintenance, not compliance with a drinking-water rule. Other reasons to pause:

  • Sodium. NDSU’s rule of thumb is about 8 mg/L of sodium added for each grain of hardness removed. Softening 7.9 gpg all the way down would add on the order of 63 mg/L of sodium to the softened water, on top of whatever sodium is already in the supply (WaterOne’s 2025-data average sodium was 56 ppm). That is a dietary accounting issue, especially for people on sodium-restricted diets, not a number we are going to turn into medical advice. NDSU’s practical workaround is an unsoftened kitchen tap for drinking and cooking, or potassium chloride as the regenerant, with the cost and efficiency trade-offs noted in the comparison guide.
  • Corrosion and pipe metals. WaterOne warns that the trade-off of a home softener is plumbing that is more susceptible to corrosion, with more wear and a greater possibility that heavy metals will leach into the water. The utility also says a degree of hardness helps deposit a fine layer of calcium on pipes, which is part of how it talks about protecting customers from lead in plumbing. That is a serious caution on older homes and any home with lead solder or fixtures of concern. It is a reason to involve a licensed plumber, not to ignore faucet filters where lead is the actual risk.
  • Plants and irrigation. NDSU does not recommend softened water for house plants, lawns, or gardens because of the sodium. A bypass for outside hose bibs is a standard design detail if you do install a unit.
  • The problem might not be hardness. Chlorine taste wants a carbon filter. A specific contaminant wants a filter or RO system certified for that contaminant. A salt-free conditioner will not make 8 gpg water test soft. Match the device to the job.

Middle paths that are not “buy a softener or do nothing”

  • Descale kettles, coffee makers, and shower heads on a schedule. USGS even mentions vinegar in a coffee maker as a way to dissolve mineral deposits.
  • Check the water-heater temperature. WaterOne says that in most cases of calcium buildup it has seen discussed in this context, the tank was set higher than normal.
  • Use a point-of-use carbon filter or RO system at the kitchen sink if taste or dissolved solids in drinking water are the real complaint, and leave the rest of the house alone.
  • If scale on the heater and pipes is the only goal, a salt-free conditioner is sometimes sold as a middle path. Remember that it does not produce soft water and is not proven to reach less than 1 gpg; read the comparison guide before treating it as a softener substitute.

A practical way to decide

  1. Confirm your number. For WaterOne customers right now, that is about 7.9 gpg from the latest average, not a guess.
  2. List the problems you actually have (scale, soap, spots, taste, a specific contaminant).
  3. If the problems are mild and the water is city-treated at about 8 gpg, trying maintenance first is consistent with the extension guidance above.
  4. If you do want whole-house softening, plan a drinking-water bypass, outdoor bypass, salt or potassium supply, and a corrosion conversation in older plumbing — and size the unit from total hardness, not from a sales script.
  5. Do not buy from a door-to-door pitch that treats 8 gpg as an emergency. Hardness at this level is common, documented, and not a health-code violation.
